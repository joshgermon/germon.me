# 14-11-26

## I put the FAA's nationwide ground stop on a globe

I've always loved airplanes and I can't really walk past a flight tracker without stopping to look at it. A while back I built a little ESP32 with one of those cheap yellow displays that shows whichever plane is currently overhead, and I've wanted to do more with flight data since then.

I'm in Australia, so it was Saturday morning when I heard the FAA had stopped every departure in the US (3:54 p.m. to 8:16 p.m. Eastern on the Friday). I found the whole thing fascinating and spent a good chunk of the morning on Hacker News reading the theories about what had happened.

What I was more curious about though was the flight data itself, and how that evening would compare to a normal Friday. So I built [Ground stop](/experiments/ground-stop/) to have a look.

### The data

Planes broadcast their own position over ADS-B and volunteers around the world run receivers that pick it up. [adsb.lol](https://www.adsb.lol) publishes all of that as open data, so the evening is built from their history files, which have every airline flight sampled once a minute.

To compare I used the Friday before, for the same hours and from the same source. The coverage isn't perfect (it's thin over the oceans, so my counts are lower than the official ones) but that applies to both Fridays equally, so I think the comparison is still fair. Airline flights over the US went from around 2,400 to under 100.

The times for when things happened are from the FAA's own advisories.

### The live part

I also wanted it to keep showing the sky after the event, so the site now records where flights are every few minutes. That took a lot longer than I expected. Most of the sources I tried don't respond to requests coming from Cloudflare, which is where this site runs. adsb.lol does, but it only allows about a dozen requests every 75 seconds or so, and covering the US takes 30 of them.

So the live view updates every four minutes rather than every two like I originally wanted. It's a free service run by volunteers though, so I'm not going to complain about that.

The numbers on the page are all either measured from this data or have the source next to them.

### What it looks like

The thing I found most interesting when I first played it back is that the sky doesn't clear out when the stop starts. The flights already in the air keep going and land over the next few hours, so the quietest point is actually at 8:39 p.m., about 20 minutes after the stop had been lifted. You can also click on an airport to see how many departures it lost compared to the Friday before, and Chicago O'Hare was the worst of them.

You can have a look at it here: [germon.me/experiments/ground-stop](/experiments/ground-stop/)
